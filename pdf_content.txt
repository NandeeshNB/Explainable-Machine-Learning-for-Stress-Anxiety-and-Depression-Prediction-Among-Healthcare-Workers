ABSTRACT
Mental health is a significant concern among healthcare workers due to demanding workloads, long
working hours, high patient responsibility, irregular shifts, emotionally challenging situations and
continuous exposure to stressful clinical environments. Prolonged occupational stress can contribute
to burnout and may also be associated with anxiety and depression. Therefore, identifying elevated
mental-health risk among healthcare workers at an early stage can support awareness and preventive
intervention.
This project, Mental Health Prediction Using Machine Learning for Healthcare Workers, extends an
existing machine-learning-based stress and burnout prediction system developed during the previous
semester. The previous system primarily focused on stress and burnout risk using physiological,
environmental and occupational information. The proposed project expands the scope toward
broader mental-health risk prediction by incorporating the DASS-42 dataset, which provides
psychological indicators associated with depression, anxiety and stress, and by incorporating an
additional healthcare workforce mental-health dataset that provides workforce-level context.
The proposed framework utilizes four complementary datasets: the Stress-Lysis dataset, the
healthcare workplace survey dataset, the DASS-42 dataset and the Healthcare Workforce Mental
Health dataset. The Stress-Lysis dataset contributes physical and environmental information such as
temperature, humidity and activity level. The workplace survey contributes occupational information
including weekly working hours, patients per shift, department and stress score. DASS-42 contributes
psychological information through its depression, anxiety and stress questionnaire responses. The
workforce dataset contributes workplace stressor and workforce mental-health outcome information
for organization-level analysis and robustness experiments.
Because these datasets originate from different sources and do not represent the same individuals,
they will not be directly concatenated row-by-row. Instead, the project will develop a unified multi-
source feature representation in which physical, environmental, occupational, psychological and
workforce information is standardized and transformed into complementary features for the mental-
health prediction framework.
Five machine-learning algorithms will be implemented and compared: Decision Tree, Random Forest,
XGBoost, LightGBM and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). Their performance will be evaluated using
accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrices and other appropriate classification metrics.

=== PAGE 2 ===
The evaluation is further extended with probability calibration (reliability diagrams, Brier score,
Expected Calibration Error), fairness assessment using Fairlearn, class-imbalance experiments using
class weighting and SMOTE, and ablation studies that quantify the contribution of each dataset,
feature group and methodological component.
To improve interpretability, SHAP-based Explainable AI will be used to identify the factors contributing
to individual and overall predictions, and DiCE will be used to generate counterfactual "what-if"
explanations. A Generative AI component, grounded through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG),
will then convert model predictions and feature explanations into understandable mental-health risk
summaries for healthcare workers, together with an organization-oriented workforce report.
The existing Python, Jupyter Notebook and Streamlit architecture will be extended rather than
replaced with a separate frontend, backend or database system, with Plotly added for interactive
analytics and MLflow as an optional experiment-tracking layer. The final system will serve as an
experimental and educational framework for mental-health risk prediction among healthcare workers
and will not be intended as a clinical diagnostic system.
CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background
Healthcare workers operate in environments that can involve high workloads, long working hours,
emergency situations, irregular schedules, patient responsibility and emotionally demanding
interactions. These conditions can contribute to elevated occupational stress and may affect overall
psychological well-being.
Mental health among healthcare workers is therefore an important area for research. Stress, anxiety
and depression are interconnected but distinct dimensions of psychological well-being. A system that
focuses only on stress or burnout may not capture the broader mental-health profile of a healthcare
worker.
Machine learning provides an opportunity to identify patterns in structured data and estimate the
likelihood of different mental-health risk categories. However, a prediction alone is not sufficient for a
responsible system. The predicted probability should be reliable, the errors should be examined across
groups, and the reasoning behind the prediction should be explainable.
The proposed project focuses specifically on healthcare workers and extends the existing stress and
burnout prediction system into a broader, responsible mental-health prediction framework.

1.4 Motivation
Healthcare workers frequently work under demanding conditions. Workload, working hours, patient
volume and environmental factors may influence stress levels, while psychological factors can provide
additional information regarding anxiety and depression.
The previous project primarily concentrated on stress and burnout. However, mental health is broader
than these two outcomes.
Therefore, the proposed project introduces DASS-42 to provide psychological information associated
with depression, anxiety and stress, and a workforce dataset to provide organization-level context
such as workplace stressors and workforce mental-health outcomes.
A second motivation is responsibility. A mental-health risk system that outputs a probability should be
evaluated for whether that probability is meaningful, and whether its errors are distributed unevenly
across groups. The project therefore aims to investigate not only how accurately the models predict,
but also how reliably, how fairly and how understandably.
1.5 Problem Statement
Healthcare workers may experience substantial occupational and psychological stress due to long
working hours, high patient workloads, demanding clinical environments and emotionally challenging
situations.
Existing systems may focus on individual factors or a single mental-health outcome, and often report
only predictive accuracy without examining probability reliability, group-level disparities or the
contribution of individual components.
The revised problem is therefore to design and experimentally evaluate a unified, explainable and
responsible machine-learning framework that can learn from complementary physical/environmental,
occupational, psychological and workforce information, while explicitly accounting for data-source
differences, class imbalance, calibration, fairness and uncertainty.

1.6 Aim
To develop and evaluate an explainable, fair, calibrated and evidence-grounded multi-source machine-
learning framework for predicting stress, anxiety and depression risk among healthcare workers using
physical, environmental, occupational, psychological and workforce information.

5.1 Predictive Performance Metrics
Accuracy — measures overall classification correctness.
$$Accuracy = \frac{Correct\ Predictions}{Total\ Predictions}$$
Precision — measures how many predicted positive cases are actually positive.
$$Precision = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}$$
Recall — measures how many actual positive cases are correctly identified.
$$Recall = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$
F1 Score — balances precision and recall.
$$F1 = 2 \times \frac{Precision \times Recall}{Precision + Recall}$$
Confusion Matrix — confusion matrices will be generated for each prediction task.
ROC-AUC — ROC-AUC will be used where the target formulation makes it appropriate.

CHAPTER 6 — PROBABILITY CALIBRATION (New Chapter)
Calibration is added because a risk system should distinguish between a ranking model and a
probability estimate. A model producing 0.80 risk should be tested for whether cases receiving
approximately 0.80 probability actually exhibit the outcome at roughly that frequency in a suitable
evaluation population.
6.1 Methods
Reliability diagram — compare predicted probability bins with observed event frequency.
Brier score — evaluate probabilistic prediction error.
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) — summarize the gap between predicted confidence and observed
frequency across bins.
Platt/sigmoid scaling or isotonic regression — investigate post-hoc calibration using validation data
only.
Report calibration before and after calibration rather than assuming calibration automatically
improves.
6.2 Evaluation Protocol
Calibration will be evaluated separately for each target, because stress, anxiety and depression may
have different class distributions and model behavior.
Calibration will be fitted on a validation split and reported on the held-out test split.
Calibration will be re-measured after class balancing, since resampling can distort predicted
probabilities.
Instability of calibration estimates on small test sets will be explicitly acknowledged.

CHAPTER 7 — FAIRNESS AND BIAS MITIGATION (New Chapter)
The original proposal identified bias as an ethical consideration and stated that potential dataset
biases should be acknowledged. The expanded framework operationalizes this requirement with
explicit measurement and mitigation using Fairlearn.
7.1 Fairness Workflow
Identify demographic/contextual attributes actually available and appropriate for analysis in the
chosen datasets.
Evaluate whether sample sizes are sufficient for meaningful group comparisons.
Measure group-specific recall, false-positive rate, false-negative rate and related fairness statistics.
Use Fairlearn to visualize and compare performance across groups.
Where justified, investigate mitigation such as reweighting, threshold optimization or other
constrained approaches.
Compare baseline versus mitigated models across performance, calibration and fairness together,
rather than optimizing one metric in isolation.
7.2 Constraints on the Analysis
Fairness analysis must not invent demographic variables that are absent from the source data. If a
dataset does not contain an appropriate sensitive attribute, the report will state clearly that fairness
for that dimension could not be evaluated.
Where group sample sizes are too small for stable estimates, the report will present the result as
indicative only.
7.3 Reported Outputs
Group-wise performance table
Fairness gap metrics (e.g. equalized-odds difference, demographic parity difference)

8.5 DiCE Counterfactual Explanations (New Addition)
DiCE adds a complementary explanation question. Instead of only asking why the model predicted a
given risk, the system can investigate what feasible input changes would alter the model's prediction.
Generate counterfactual examples for suitable tabular models.
Apply feasibility constraints so unrealistic changes are not suggested.
Separate actionable variables (e.g. weekly work hours, activity level) from immutable/contextual
variables (e.g. department, demographic attributes).
Present counterfactuals as model-based scenarios, not medical prescriptions.
Example format
Current prediction: Stress Risk = High
Counterfactual scenario:
Weekly work hours: 64 → 52
Patients per shift: 18 → 14
→ Predicted Stress Risk = Moderate
(Model-based what-if scenario only.
Not a causal claim or a clinical recommendation.)
8.6 Explanation Handoff Rule
Only model-generated evidence — prediction, calibrated probability, SHAP contributions and DiCE
counterfactuals — is passed into the reporting layer. The reporting layer does not compute or modify
risk.

CHAPTER 17 — LIMITATIONS
17.1 Different Data Sources
The four datasets originate from different sources and populations. Therefore, they should not be
interpreted as one naturally collected healthcare-worker cohort, and they do not represent a
longitudinal cohort.
17.2 Different Feature Spaces
The datasets measure different aspects of human behavior and mental health. Therefore, feature
engineering and normalization are required before constructing the unified framework.
17.3 DASS-42 Dataset Population
DASS-42 itself is a psychological assessment instrument and is not inherently restricted to healthcare
workers. Therefore, the report should not falsely claim that every DASS-42 respondent is a healthcare
worker. Instead, DASS-42 serves as the psychological component of the framework, while the project's
application domain remains healthcare workers.
17.4 Synthetic Fourth Dataset
The Healthcare Workforce Mental Health dataset may be synthetic. If so, its results must not be
interpreted as real-world prevalence or clinical evidence, and its role is restricted to methodological
and workforce-analysis experiments.
17.5 Dataset Shift
Different source distributions can create dataset shift, which may affect generalization across sources.
17.6 Self-Reported Information
DASS-42 is based on self-reported responses, which may introduce reporting bias.

=== PAGE 37 ===
17.7 Fairness Limitations
Fairness analysis is limited by the sensitive attributes actually present in the data and by the sample
sizes available for each group.
17.8 Calibration Limitations
Calibration estimates may be unstable for small test sets, and calibration fitted on one source may not
transfer to another.
17.9 Resampling Limitations
SMOTE can create synthetic minority samples that do not necessarily correspond to real individuals.
17.10 Counterfactual Limitations
Counterfactuals are model-based what-if scenarios, not guaranteed causal effects.
17.11 Generative AI Limitations
RAG does not make a report medically authoritative; retrieved evidence quality remains important.
Generative AI may hallucinate unless tightly constrained and evaluated.
17.12 No Clinical Diagnosis
The system is not clinically validated and should not be used to diagnose mental-health disorders.
CHAPTER 18 — ETHICAL AND RESPONSIBLE-AI CONSIDERATIONS
Privacy — Personal and identifiable information should not be unnecessarily collected or exposed.
Responsible Prediction — Predictions must be presented as risk estimates rather than diagnoses.
Transparency — The system should explain the factors influencing predictions, and show uncertainty
and limitations where appropriate.
Bias — Potential biases within datasets should be measured where possible and acknowledged where
measurement is not possible. Fairness mitigation must not be used to conceal poor data quality.
Generative AI — The Generative AI system should explain model outputs rather than make medical
claims, and must not override model outputs. Model predictions, explanations and retrieved evidence
must remain distinguishable in the report.
Aggregation — Use aggregate organization analytics where individual privacy or small sample sizes
make individual reporting inappropriate.
Documentation — Synthetic-data usage must be documented explicitly.

=== PAGE 38 ===
Honest Reporting — Negative results and trade-offs must be reported rather than selecting only
favorable experiments.
No Guaranteed Outcomes — Do not generate recommendations that imply guaranteed clinical
outcomes.
